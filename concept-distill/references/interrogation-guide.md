# Interrogation Guide

Patterns for sharpening each of the six fields — **not a script to read aloud.**

**How to use this bank:**
- **Never ask a question verbatim.** Instantiate it on the user's own material:
  quote what they (or a source) said, then ask. "You said '<their words>' — does
  that mean A or B?" A question that doesn't quote their material is too generic;
  rewrite it until it does.
- **The sharp answer must come from them.** The *thin → sharp* examples below show
  what a good answer sounds like — they are **targets to elicit, never text for you
  to write on the user's behalf.** If you find yourself composing the sharp version,
  stop and ask the question that makes the user say it.
- **Recommend, don't invent.** When you offer a recommended answer, draw it from
  something they already said or a source you can quote — not a plausible guess.
- Ask **one at a time**. Converge — each answer closes a field, it does not open
  three. Stop a field when its answer would **force a design decision**.

---

## 1. Single job

- "In one sentence, what does this do that nothing else does?"
- "If a user could only do one thing here, what is it?"
- Push: if the sentence lists two jobs, ask which one the other serves.

*Thin*: "helps people manage their tasks." *Sharp*: "shows you the one task to do
now and nothing else, so the future stops being noise."

## 2. Audience & state

- "Who reaches for this, and **what are they feeling** in that moment?"
- "What just happened to them right before they open it?"
- Push past demographics: "25–34" tells us nothing; "overwhelmed, behind, avoiding
  the app that nags them" forces calm, non-nagging design.

*Thin*: "busy professionals." *Sharp*: "someone who feels behind and a little
ashamed, opening it hoping not to be judged."

## 3. Values (→ forced axioms)

- "Name 2–3 things you'd protect even at the cost of growth or polish."
- For each: "What does that value **forbid** you from doing?" (A value that forbids
  nothing isn't load-bearing.)
- Push: if they list five, ask which two they'd keep if forced to drop the rest.

*Thin*: "we value trust." *Sharp*: "honesty — we'd show 'not enough data' before a
comforting fake number." (Forbids: fabricated certainty.)

## 4. Subject world

- "What does this domain *call* things? Give me the insider words."
- "What are the physical or conceptual materials here — the instruments,
  artifacts, metaphors people in this world already use?"
- This is where distinctiveness comes from; mine it even if the user thinks it's
  obvious.

*Thin*: "it's a finance app." *Sharp*: "ledgers, reconciliation, the close, ruled
columns, red and black, the audit trail."

## 5. Forbidden moves

- "What would be *wrong* for this product to do, even if it boosted a metric?"
- "What do competitors do that you refuse to?"
- Tie each to a value: "ruled out by which of your values?"

*Thin*: "nothing comes to mind." *Sharp*: "no urgency banners or streak-guilt —
ruled out by 'we don't manipulate anxious users.'"

## 6. Content shape

- "What is the core content, really — a list, a sequence, a distribution, one
  thing at a time?"
- "Does order matter to the user, or is it a set?"
- Push: the *true* shape drives layout. A sequence earns numbering; a distribution
  wants a chart, not a flowchart; one-at-a-time forbids an enumerated list.

*Thin*: "we show their data." *Sharp*: "one outcome at a time, swipeable — never a
list, because seeing all of it at once overwhelms them."

---

## Convergence checks (when to stop)

- Every field's answer names a **consequence**, not a restatement.
- You could derive a non-generic design from the set (the downstream test).
- The whole thing could only describe *this* product.
- Asking another question wouldn't change any derived decision → stop. Sufficiency
  beats exhaustiveness.
